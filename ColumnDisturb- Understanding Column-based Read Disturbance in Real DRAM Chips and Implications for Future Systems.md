gist: Demonstrates and characterizes ColumnDisturb, a column-based (bitline-mediated) DRAM read disturbance phenomenon in which hammering or pressing a single aggressor row induces 1-to-0 bitflips in cells sharing its columns across three consecutive subarrays (all 3072 rows in tested DDR4) rather than in a few adjacent rows, showing across 216 DDR4 and 4 HBM2 chips from all three vendors that it already flips bits within the nominal 64 ms refresh window in one existing module (63.6 ms, 85 C), worsens with technology scaling and temperature, affects up to 198x more rows than retention failures, and thereby erases most of the performance and energy benefit of retention-aware heterogeneous refresh mechanisms such as RAIDR.

# ColumnDisturb: Understanding Column-based Read Disturbance in Real DRAM Chips and Implications for Future Systems

1. problem (Heilmeier)
	1. FACT: DRAM read disturbance - "reading data from a memory or storage device causes physical disturbance ... on another piece of data that is *not* accessed but located physically nearby the accessed data" - breaks memory isolation, "a fundamental building block of robust (i.e., safe, secure, reliable, available) systems", and worsens with every technology node shrink (Sec. 1, Sec. 2.2)
	2. FACT: the field's entire model of read disturbance is **row-adjacency-scoped**. RowHammer and RowPress both "affect *a few* rows within the same subarray", so every deployed and proposed mitigation is built to refresh a small neighborhood - "current RowHammer mitigations only refresh up to 8 neighboring rows" (Sec. 1, Sec. 6.1)
	3. FACT: the gap this paper attacks - a DRAM row activation drives its content onto *every column (bitline) in the subarray*, and in the open-bitline architecture those bitlines are shared with two neighboring subarrays. Nobody had asked what that does to the cells hanging off those columns. The paper's stated aim is to "discover and analyze *previously unknown* read disturbance phenomena" (Sec. 1)
	4. FACT: the second problem, on the reliability side - retention-aware heterogeneous refresh mechanisms [75-88] extract their benefit from the *proportion of strong rows* (rows that hold data far past 64 ms). If a second, non-retention mechanism makes rows fail at long refresh intervals, that proportion collapses and a decade of refresh-optimization work loses its premise (Sec. 6.2)
	5. [INFERENCE] the deeper framing problem: RowHammer mitigation cost is budgeted as O(a few victim rows per aggressor). If the true victim set is O(a subarray x 3), the *cost model* of the entire mitigation literature is wrong by three orders of magnitude, not the mechanisms themselves
2. challenge
	1. FACT: the effect is **invisible unless you first subtract two known phenomena**. ColumnDisturb bitflips appear at intervals longer than tREFW, so retention failures must be filtered out; and they appear in the aggressor's subarray, where RowHammer & RowPress also flip bits. Both confounds must be removed before any claim survives (Sec. 3.2)
	2. FACT: physical subarray boundaries are not exposed by any interface, and "characterizing and understanding ColumnDisturb requires ... reverse engineering the subarray boundaries because the columns of a single DRAM row span three physically consecutive subarrays" (Sec. 3.2)
	3. FACT: in-DRAM logical-to-physical row remapping must be reverse engineered per module before "physically adjacent" means anything (Sec. 3.1)
	4. FACT: the measurement takes a long time by construction - the bisection search for the minimum hammer count is repeated five times per subarray, terminates only after 512 ms of no bitflips, and is run over **all subarrays in all banks of all tested modules** (46,080 subarrays for Fig. 6 alone) (Sec. 3.2, Sec. 4.1)
	5. FACT: on-die defenses and periodic refresh mask the device-level behavior, so periodic refresh is disabled and only modules "with neither rank-level nor on-die ECC" are used - which then makes the characterization non-representative of a deployed system (Sec. 3.2)
	6. [INFERENCE] the hardest scientific challenge is *attribution*, not detection: a 1-to-0 bitflip at a 512 ms refresh interval looks exactly like a retention failure. Everything in Sec. 3.2 exists to make the two separable, and the paper's credibility rests on that separation more than on any single number
3. current SoTA (Heilmeier)
	1. FACT: **RowHammer** [2, 3] as the canonical model - repeatedly activating an aggressor row flips bits in physically nearby victim rows; the severity metric is a per-chip activation threshold. Limit targeted: victims are assumed to be *immediate row neighbors in one subarray* (Sec. 1, Sec. 2.2)
	2. FACT: **RowPress** [4] - the same lab's prior work; keeping a row open (pressing, tAggOn >> tRAS) rather than hammering it induces bitflips at far fewer activations. Limit targeted: still row-adjacency-scoped, still a *row* phenomenon (Sec. 1, Sec. 2.2)
	3. FACT: **industry refresh-based mitigations** [41, 47, 147-149, 157] refresh "a few neighbor rows (e.g., up to +4/-4 in industry solutions)" of the aggressor. Limit targeted: the scope of the victim set (Sec. 4.1, Sec. 6.1). The DDR5 standard [148] prices this directly - refreshing +/-4 rows costs tDRFMab = 560 ns versus 280 ns for +/-2
	4. FACT: **retention-aware heterogeneous refresh** [75-88], e.g. RAIDR [76], ArchShield [77], AVATAR - classify rows as *weak* (fail within a short window, e.g. 64 ms) or *strong* (survive e.g. 1024 ms) and refresh strong rows rarely. Limit targeted: the classification assumes retention failures are the only long-interval failure mode (Sec. 6.2)
	5. FACT: **ECC** - SECDED [163, 168-171] at the system level and low-cost on-DRAM-die single-error-correcting (136,128) Hamming codes [160] in DDR5-class chips. Limit targeted: both assume at most one or two bitflips per codeword (Sec. 5.6)
	6. FACT: **prior read-disturbance characterization studies** sweep ten axes - refresh rate, aggressor-victim distance, DRAM generation and technology node, temperature, aggressor on time, victim cell location, wordline voltage, supply voltage, timing parameters, and time. "None of these works analyze or demonstrate ColumnDisturb, a column-based read disturbance phenomenon" (Sec. 7). Limit targeted: every axis is a *row* axis
	7. FACT: **bitline disturbance in 4F-squared VCT DRAM** [259-263] is the closest-looking prior art - emerging high-density vertical-channel-transistor arrays are known to be disturbed from the bitline under a hammering-like pattern. Limit targeted: different device architecture (4F-squared VCT vs contemporary COTS 6F-squared) and a believed-different mechanism (floating body effect), so it says nothing about commodity DRAM (Sec. 7)
	8. FACT: **no ColumnDisturb mitigation existed at publication time** - the paper proposes the first two and "call[s] for more research on the topic" (Sec. 1, Sec. 6)
4. novelty (Heilmeier)
	1. **Introduces and names ColumnDisturb**, the first experimentally demonstrated *column-based* (bitline-based) read disturbance phenomenon in commodity DRAM - "it is possible to disturb DRAM cells through a DRAM column (i.e., bitline) and induce bitflips in DRAM cells sharing the same columns as the aggressor row (across multiple DRAM subarrays)" (Abstract, Sec. 1)
		1. CLEVER: the reframing is the contribution. Read disturbance stops being a question of *which rows are near the aggressor* and becomes a question of *which cells share the aggressor's bitlines* - which, under the open-bitline architecture, is a structural answer (three subarrays) rather than an empirical one (a few rows)
	2. CLEVER: **Establishes the phenomenon by predicting its spatial signature from the array architecture, then confirming the prediction** (Sec. 4.2), rather than by curve-fitting a sweep. Three independent structural predictions all hold:
		1. exactly three consecutive subarrays are affected and no others - subarrays that neither contain the aggressor nor are physically adjacent to it "do *not* experience any ColumnDisturb bitflips", as the open-bitline architecture requires since they share no columns with the aggressor (Obs. 4, Sec. 4.2)
		2. **1.45x more bitflips in the aggressor's own subarray than in the neighbors** - predicted, because an activation perturbs *all* bitlines in its own subarray but only *half* the bitlines in each neighbor (Obs. 5)
		3. CLEVER: **zero overlap in column indices** between the bitflipping cells of the upper neighbor (Subarray 0) and the lower neighbor (Subarray 2) - Subarray 1's even columns are shared with Subarray 0, its odd columns with Subarray 2. This is a prediction with no alternative explanation, and it is the single most convincing datum in the paper (Obs. 5, Fig. 3)
	3. **Quantifies** the phenomenon on **216 COTS DDR4 chips (28 modules)** and **4 HBM2 chips** from all three major manufacturers, across temperature, data pattern, tAggOn, access pattern, aggressor location, average column voltage, refresh interval, and spatial variation - 27 numbered observations, 12 takeaways (Sec. 1, Secs. 4-5)
	4. **Demonstrates the phenomenon already exists inside the nominal refresh window** in a shipping part: a 16 Gb F-die Micron module flips bits at **63.6 ms and 85 C**, inside DDR4's 64 ms tREFW, while retention failures on the same module need at least 512 ms (Obs. 3, Fig. 11)
	5. CLEVER: **Collapses three separate sensitivity sweeps into one explanatory variable** - the *average voltage level on the perturbed columns*, AVG(V_COL) = (tAggOn x DP_COL + VDD/2 x tRP) / (tAggOn + tRP) (Sec. 4.6). Aggressor data pattern (Sec. 4.4), aggressor-on-time (Sec. 4.5), and single- vs two-aggressor access pattern (Sec. 5.3) all reduce to "how low is the column held, for how long"
		1. CLEVER: the two-aggressor experiment (Sec. 5.3) is a genuine *prediction test* of that variable rather than another sweep - complementary aggressor patterns raise the average column voltage via {GND -> VDD/2 -> VDD -> VDD/2}, and the model predicts (correctly) that this makes the chip **less** vulnerable, 1.83x-2.16x slower to the first bitflip (Obs. 21)
	6. **Establishes the failure direction is exclusively 1-to-0** (Obs. 7) - the same direction as retention failures and unlike RowHammer & RowPress, which flip both ways. This is mechanism evidence (a leakage process, not charge injection), and it is *also* the paper's own biggest attribution problem (see `limitation`)
	7. **Proposes a device-level Key Hypothesis**: ColumnDisturb "exacerbates subthreshold leakage of the access transistor [112] and/or the dielectric leakage between the capacitor and the bitline [2]" (Sec. 4.6)
	8. **Quantifies the system-level consequence for retention-aware refresh** (Sec. 6.2) - not just "there is a new failure mode" but "an existing, well-studied optimization class loses 53 percentage points of its benefit". This is the contribution with the clearest downstream cost attached
	9. **Describes and evaluates two mitigations**: increasing the DRAM refresh rate (evaluated, expensive) and PRVR, Proactively Refreshing ColumnDisturb Victim Rows (analytically estimated) (Sec. 6.1)
	10. closest prior work and the delta:
		1. vs **RowPress [4]** (same lab, four shared authors): ColumnDisturb *reuses* RowPress's tAggOn axis as a parameter but is a different phenomenon - different victim set (3072 rows across three subarrays vs +/-1 row), different direction (1-to-0 only vs both), and it is explicitly filtered out of the measurement via an 8-row guardband
		2. vs **RowHammer characterization studies [3, 4, 7, 71, 124-126, ...]**: same infrastructure lineage and metric style, but the swept variable is *which cells share a bitline*, an axis none of them tested
		3. vs **4F-squared VCT bitline disturbance [259-263]**: demonstrated on commodity 6F-squared DDR4/HBM2 rather than an emerging array architecture, and attributed to a different (leakage, not floating-body) mechanism
5. proposal (Heilmeier)
	1. core idea: **a DRAM row activation is a subarray-wide event, so treat the column as the disturbance channel.** Drive one aggressor row's content onto every bitline in its subarray, hold or toggle those bitlines, and every cell connected to them - including cells hundreds or thousands of rows away and cells in the two physically adjacent subarrays that share bitlines through the open-bitline architecture - leaks charge faster. Victims are *columns*, not neighbors
	2. **why it works** (Sec. 2.1, Sec. 4.2, Sec. 4.6): in the open-bitline architecture [92-100] each subarray's bitlines connect to two rows of sense amplifiers, one above and one below, so a subarray's *even* bitlines are shared with the subarray above and its *odd* bitlines with the subarray below. An ACT drives the aggressor row's data onto all of its subarray's bitlines and half of each neighbor's. Holding a bitline at GND (aggressor data all-0) while a victim cell holds VDD (all-1) maximizes the cell-to-bitline voltage difference, which the paper hypothesizes exacerbates access-transistor subthreshold leakage and/or capacitor-to-bitline dielectric leakage - so the victim cell bleeds toward 0. Hence 1-to-0 only, hence "more logic-0 in the perturbed columns means more bitflips", hence "lower average column voltage means more vulnerable"
	3. **the characterization mechanism** (Sec. 3) - argument chain: to claim a *new* phenomenon you must show the bitflips are neither retention nor RowHammer/RowPress, and that their spatial extent matches column sharing rather than row adjacency
		1. infrastructure: DRAM Bender [118, 119] (built on SoftMC [120, 121]) on a Xilinx Alveo U200 FPGA [122], host machine, heater pads plus temperature sensor, MaxWell FT200 controller [123] for 45/65/85/95 C (Sec. 3.1, Figs. 4-5)
		2. access pattern: ACT R_Agg --tAggOn--> PRE --tRP--> ACT R_Agg --> ... with R_Agg the *middle* row of the tested subarray; hammering raises and lowers the column voltage, pressing keeps it consistently high or low (Sec. 3.2)
		3. CLEVER: **subarray boundaries reverse-engineered via RowClone** [90] - repeatedly perform the two-consecutive-activation in-DRAM copy for every source/destination row pair per bank; when the destination ends up holding the source's content, the two rows are in the same subarray. An in-DRAM compute primitive repurposed as a microscope for array structure (Sec. 3.2)
		4. **retention filtering**: state-of-the-art retention time test methodology [84-86, 117] across five data patterns, each test repeated 50 times to cover variable retention time [85, 86, 115, 116], conclusions drawn from the lowest observed retention time; those cells are excluded (Sec. 3.2)
		5. **RowHammer/RowPress filtering**: exclude the **eight nearest physical victim rows** of the aggressor - six more than the +/-1 rows where the authors actually observe RowHammer/RowPress bitflips, as a guardband matching industry solutions that refresh up to eight nearest rows [41, 47, 147-149] (Sec. 3.2, fn. 5)
		6. **other confounds removed**: periodic refresh disabled; modules verified to have neither rank-level nor on-die ECC [134, 150, 151]; in-DRAM logical-to-physical row mapping reverse engineered (Sec. 3.1, Sec. 3.2)
		7. metrics: (1) minimum time to induce the first ColumnDisturb bitflip in a subarray, (2) fraction of cells with bitflips in a subarray, (3) blast radius = number of rows with at least one bitflip (Sec. 3.2)
		8. search: bisection on hammer count, terminating when successive minima differ by <1%; no REF for 512 ms; abandon a subarray with no bitflips in 512 ms; five iterations per subarray, hammer count converted to time (Sec. 3.2)
		9. default operating point (the most vulnerable one, found by the sweeps): aggressor data pattern all-0, victim all-1, tAggOn = 70.2 us, 85 C (Sec. 3.2, Sec. 5)
	4. **mitigation 1 - increase the DRAM refresh rate** (Sec. 6.1): set the refresh period short enough that no ColumnDisturb bitflip manifests. Deployable today, needs no new hardware. Rejected on cost - for a 32 Gb DDR5 chip, cutting REFab from 32 ms to 8 ms raises periodic-refresh throughput loss from 10.5% to **42.1%** and idle-chip refresh energy share from 25.1% to **67.5%**
	5. **mitigation 2 - PRVR (Proactively Refreshing ColumnDisturb Victim Rows)** (Sec. 6.1): refresh *only* the N victim rows across the three affected subarrays (N = 3072 for a 1024-row subarray) **once**, before the aggressor has been hammered or pressed long enough to induce a bitflip, distributing those refreshes over the time-to-first-bitflip the way periodic refresh distributes REF over tREFW
		1. **why it works**: the victim set is large but *known and structural* (three subarrays around the aggressor), and it only has to be covered once per time-to-first-bitflip window rather than every tREFW. So the cost scales with the ColumnDisturb threshold, not with the refresh window
		2. **why reactive refresh cannot work**: refreshing 3072 rows on demand costs an estimated **~215 us** (70 ns per row from DDR5's tDRFMab = 560 ns for eight rows, x 3072), a "prohibitive" memory access latency (Sec. 6.1, fn. 14)
		3. explicitly *not* engineered here - "System integration and rigorous evaluation of PRVR (e.g., using worst-case ColumnDisturb access patterns) is out of this paper's scope" (fn. 17)
	6. **the retention-aware refresh evaluation** (Sec. 6.2) - argument chain: retention-aware refresh profits from the proportion of *strong* rows, ColumnDisturb turns strong rows weak, therefore the profit disappears
		1. RAIDR [76] built on the Self-Managing DRAM (SMD) framework [248, 249], simulated in Ramulator [250-253], two variants: space-efficient (8 Kb Bloom filter [254-256], 6 hash functions) and space-inefficient (bitmap, 1 bit per row, 2 Mb for a 16 GiB DDR4 module)
		2. weak rows refreshed every 64 ms, strong rows every 1024 ms; compared against a hypothetical *No Refresh* configuration for headroom
		3. workloads: 20 highly-memory-intensive four-core multiprogrammed mixes from single-core workloads with last-level-cache MPKI >= 10, 100 M warm-up instructions, >= 500 M instructions per core
6. evaluation
	1. **scope** (Table 1): **216 DDR4 chips in 28 modules** - SK Hynix 80 chips (10 modules H0-H9, 8/16 Gb, die revs A/C/D), Micron 88 chips (12 modules M0-M11, 4/8/16 Gb, die revs B/E/F/R), Samsung 48 chips (6 modules S0-S5, 4/16 Gb, die revs A/B/C) - plus **4 Samsung HBM2 chips** (density and die revision not publicly available)
	2. **universality** (Obs. 1): **all 216 tested DDR4 chips are vulnerable**; every chip has at least one cell that experiences a ColumnDisturb bitflip
	3. **technology scaling** (Obs. 2, Fig. 6, over all 46,080 tested subarrays): minimum time to first bitflip falls with newer die revisions - SK Hynix **5.06x** (8 Gb A-die to D-die) and 1.29x (16 Gb A to C), Micron **2.98x** (16 Gb B to F), Samsung **2.50x** (16 Gb A to C). Headline in the abstract: "up to 5.06x and 2.96x on average across all tested DRAM modules". Caveat: die-revision letter order is only a *proxy* for node size (fn. 3)
	4. **already inside the refresh window** (Obs. 3, Fig. 11): in **one** 16 Gb F-die Micron module, multiple cells across **at least three chips** flip at **63.6 ms, 85 C** - inside DDR4's nominal tREFW - whereas retention failures on that module need >= 512 ms at 85 C. The closest (farthest) in-window victim row is **374 (446) rows away** from the aggressor, in the same subarray
	5. **spatial extent** (Obs. 4-5, Fig. 2, representative Samsung 16 Gb A-die S0; aggressor row 1536 in Subarray 1, boundaries at 0-1023 / 1024-2047 / 2048-3071): **all 3072 rows** across three consecutive subarrays experience ColumnDisturb bitflips, while only rows 1535 and 1537 experience RowHammer & RowPress bitflips
		1. magnitude separation at those two neighbor rows: **7559 RowHammer** and **5406 RowPress** bitflips on average, versus ColumnDisturb's **2353-3505** bitflips per row across all other victim rows - so the two phenomena are distinguishable by magnitude as well as by location
		2. non-adjacent subarrays show **no** ColumnDisturb bitflips, exactly as the open-bitline architecture predicts
		3. aggressor subarray gets **1.45x** more bitflips than the neighboring subarrays; the two neighbors' bitflipping cells have **no overlap in column index** with each other
		4. direction check (Obs. 7, Fig. 7): across all tested refresh intervals and subarrays, **only 1-to-0** bitflips are observed - zero 0-to-1 - unlike RowHammer & RowPress, which the authors observe in both directions on the same chips
	6. **versus retention failures - bitflip count** (Obs. 6, 8): at a 16 s refresh interval, **2942.68** bitflips per row in the aggressor subarray and **2025.02** per row in the neighbors, i.e. **7.07x** and **4.87x** more than retention failures. At shorter intervals the gap widens: **11.77x / 7.02x / 4.86x / 3.97x / 4.58x** more at 1 / 2 / 4 / 8 / 16 s
	7. **versus retention failures - affected rows (blast radius)** (Obs. 13-14, 18, Fig. 11, Fig. 15): at a 1024 ms interval, ColumnDisturb (retention) flips bits in up to **52 (20), 353 (34), 1022 (29)** rows for SK Hynix / Micron / Samsung. At 512 ms, **2, 6, and 232** rows on average versus at most two rows for retention. Across all conditions, **up to 198x more rows** than retention failures. From 512 ms to 1024 ms, ColumnDisturb adds **9.73 / 215.85 / 159.22** rows on average versus at most 8.39 (2.76 average) for retention
	8. **abstract's headline system pairing** (Sec. 1): with a 512 ms refresh interval at **65 C**, ColumnDisturb induces **1.64x / 62.49x / 152.66x** more bitflips in **2 / 6 / 232** more rows on average for SK Hynix / Micron / Samsung than retention failures
	9. **temperature** (Obs. 16-19, Figs. 13-15): 45 C to 95 C reduces time to first bitflip by **9.05x / 5.15x / 1.96x**. ColumnDisturb is *more* temperature-sensitive than retention: SK Hynix 85 C to 95 C multiplies ColumnDisturb bitflips by **72.96x** versus retention's **3.68x**. Even at 45 C, Micron and Samsung already show bitflips in up to **39 and 150** rows while retention shows at most two (Micron) or none (Samsung). At 95 C the blast radius "nearly span[s] an entire subarray"; at 65 C, at least **5.33%** of Samsung subarrays already have a blast radius of 1000
	10. **aggressor on time** (Obs. 11, 20, Figs. 9, 16): at a 16 s interval, raising tAggOn from 36 ns to 70.2 us multiplies bitflips by **1.20x / 2.12x / 2.45x**; going 36 ns to 7.8 us cuts time-to-first-bitflip by **1.68x / 1.22x / 2.03x**, and beyond tAggOn >> tRAS the distributions converge across tAggOn values
	11. **data pattern** (Obs. 9-10, 22-23, Figs. 8, 18, 19): all-0 aggressor induces **1.15x / 11.52x / 2.86x** more bitflips than all-1 at a 16 s interval. Data pattern has only a **small (<=1.31x)** effect on *time to first bitflip* - the weakest cell dominates - but a **large** effect on *total* bitflips: a Samsung 0x00 aggressor pattern gives **2.04x** more than 0xAA. Striking corner (Obs. 10): with an all-1 aggressor *matching* the victim pattern, Micron sees **2.73x fewer** bitflipping cells than retention failures at 16 s - i.e. ColumnDisturb can vanish below the retention floor when the column-to-cell voltage difference is zero
	12. **average column voltage** (Obs. 12, Fig. 10): lowering AVG(V_COL) from VDD to GND multiplies the fraction of bitflipping cells by **1.65x / 26.31x / 7.50x** at a 16 s interval. This is the paper's unifying variable
	13. **access pattern** (Obs. 21, Fig. 17): the single-aggressor pattern induces the first bitflip **1.83x / 1.92x / 2.16x faster** than a two-aggressor complementary pattern - the prediction the AVG(V_COL) model makes
	14. **aggressor location** (Obs. 24, Fig. 20): beginning / middle / end of a subarray differ by at most **1.08x** on average - spatial variation within a subarray is not a significant factor
	15. **HBM2** (Obs. 15, Fig. 12, 4 Samsung chips, one bank): **1.61x / 2.08x / 2.43x** more bitflips than retention failures at 1 / 2 / 4 s. Explicitly framed as "a preliminary study"; the authors expect the DDR4 observations to carry over "since the DRAM array is the same for both"
	16. **ECC** (Obs. 25-27, Fig. 21): many 8-byte chunks experience **3 (up to 15)** ColumnDisturb bitflips in Micron and Samsung chips - beyond SECDED's correct-or-detect capability. Correcting 15 bitflips per 8-byte chunk would need a (7,4) Hamming code at **75%** storage overhead. A low-cost on-die (136,128) SEC Hamming code **miscorrects 88.5%** of 10 K randomly generated two-bitflip codewords, turning them into three-bitflip codewords - ECC actively makes it worse
	17. **mitigation 1 cost** (Sec. 6.1, 32 Gb DDR5): REFab 32 ms to 8 ms raises refresh throughput loss from **10.5% to 42.1%** and idle refresh energy share from **25.1% to 67.5%**
	18. **mitigation 2 benefit** (Sec. 6.1, analytical): assuming a 32 ms default refresh period, an **assumed** 8 ms time-to-first-bitflip, N = 3072, and one continuously hammered row per bank in a 32 Gb DDR5 chip, PRVR cuts the 8 ms refresh period's throughput loss by **70.5%** and refresh energy by **73.8%**
	19. **retention-aware refresh damage** (Sec. 6.2, Figs. 22-23) - the paper's most consequential system result:
		1. refresh operations needed rise by **3.02x** on average and up to **14.43x** at a 1024 ms strong-row retention time, versus a baseline with only retention failures
		2. **Bloom-filter RAIDR**: for the annotated Micron module M8, speedup and energy benefits drop by **31 and 32 percentage points**. As the weak-row proportion rises from 1.00e-04 to 0.20% (20x), the benefit is **almost completely eliminated (~99 percentage point reduction)** - and the empirically measured increase in weak-row proportion is **up to 198x**, well past that 20x
		3. **bitmap RAIDR** (high area overhead): speedup and energy benefits drop by **53 and 50 percentage points** for the same module
		4. Takeaway 12: ColumnDisturb "can completely negate the performance and energy benefits of low-area-overhead ... [and] greatly reduces the benefits of high-area-overhead retention-aware heterogeneous refresh mechanisms"
7. assumptions
	1. **Die-revision letter order stands in for technology node.** "The technology node size of a DRAM chip is usually not publicly available. Prior works [3, 4, 71, 124-126] assume that for a given chip manufacturer and chip density, the alphabetical order of die revision codes may provide an indication of technology node advancement" (fn. 3). Every "worsens as DRAM scales" claim - including Takeaway 1 and the entire forward-looking argument of Sec. 6.1 - rests on this proxy, not on measured nodes
	2. **The RowClone-based subarray boundary map is correct** (Sec. 3.2). If a boundary is wrong, "three consecutive subarrays" and "all 3072 rows" are wrong, and Obs. 4-5 - the paper's strongest structural evidence - go with it. [INFERENCE] no independent cross-check of the boundaries is reported
	3. **An 8-row guardband fully separates ColumnDisturb from RowHammer & RowPress.** The number is chosen to match what industry mitigations refresh, not derived from a measurement of the true RowHammer blast radius on these parts (Sec. 3.2, fn. 5). The paper does report that excluding only two neighbors gives "essentially the same" results, which is reassuring but is a robustness check, not a bound
	4. **The retention filter captures the worst case of variable retention time.** Five data patterns, 50 repeats, lowest observed retention time (Sec. 3.2). [INFERENCE] VRT is stochastic and long-tailed; 50 repeats bounds the risk but cannot eliminate a cell that would have failed on repeat 51 being misattributed to ColumnDisturb. This assumption is load-bearing precisely because ColumnDisturb and retention share the same 1-to-0 direction
	5. **Circuit-level behavior measured with refresh off, ECC absent, and no on-die mitigation transfers to deployed systems.** [INFERENCE] the paper never measures ColumnDisturb under periodic refresh on a real system, and unlike its RowPress predecessor it offers no real-machine demonstration at all
	6. **Bitflip behavior at 512 ms - 16 s refresh intervals extrapolates down toward the 64 ms window.** Most headline "many more than retention" ratios are measured at intervals 8x-250x beyond nominal tREFW; the claim that matters for real systems (in-window bitflips) is supported by a single module at 63.6 ms. [INFERENCE] the extrapolation is plausible given the monotone trends but is nowhere stated as an assumption

	9. **HBM2 behaves like DDR4** because "the DRAM array is the same for both, and ColumnDisturb happens at the array level" (Sec. 4.8) - asserted from four chips in a single bank, with no die revision or density information available for those chips (Table 1)
	10. **A memory controller can know when to fire PRVR.** PRVR must refresh the victim rows "once before the aggressor row is hammered or pressed enough to induce a bitflip", which presumes a known, characterized, per-device ColumnDisturb threshold and a way to track per-aggressor progress toward it. 9. impact (Heilmeier)
	1. **who cares**: DRAM vendors (SK Hynix, Micron, Samsung - all three shown vulnerable, and named rather than anonymized); memory-controller and SoC architects who must now budget a 3072-row victim set; JEDEC, since no standard defines a column-based disturbance parameter and DDR5's DRFM primitive is scoped to +/-4 rows; the RowHammer mitigation community, whose mechanisms are all row-adjacency-scoped; **the retention-aware refresh literature [75-88], whose central premise this paper falsifies**; DRAM device physicists, who now have an unexplained leakage phenomenon to model; HBM and in-memory-compute designers
	2. **what adoption changes - the mitigation cost model.** Read disturbance stops being a neighborhood problem. A mitigation that refreshes +/-4 rows (DDR5's tDRFMab = 560 ns) is not under-tuned, it is *scoped wrong* by three orders of magnitude: the correct victim set is ~3072 rows, and refreshing it reactively costs ~215 us. That forces every mitigation into the proactive/amortized regime, which is exactly the design space ColumnKeeper and CODA subsequently occupy
	3. **what it changes for the refresh literature.** Retention-aware heterogeneous refresh is the clearest casualty: its benefit is a function of the strong-row proportion, and ColumnDisturb raises the weak-row proportion by up to 198x, erasing ~99 percentage points of a Bloom-filter RAIDR's benefit and 53 of a bitmap RAIDR's. A design point that a decade of papers optimized turns out to be optimizing against the wrong failure model
	4. **what it changes for defenders' fallbacks.** ECC is removed as an answer twice over: SECDED cannot cover 3-15 bitflips per 8-byte chunk, and a low-cost on-die SEC code *miscorrects 88.5%* of two-bitflip codewords into three-bitflip ones - i.e. the cheap ECC everyone assumes is a safety net actively amplifies the damage
	5. **external evidence it landed** (verified outside the paper): selected as one of the twelve **IEEE Micro Top Picks 2026** papers; two independent mitigation follow-ons within a year - **ColumnKeeper** (Kakolyris, Bostancı, Olgun, Yüksel, Songara, Sgouras, Baser, Kanellopoulos, Yağlıkçı, Mutlu; ISCA 2026; arXiv:2606.22632; artifact available/functional/reproduced, MIT-licensed) and **CODA** (Moinuddin Qureshi; arXiv:2607.13505, 2026). CODA's framing - that naively extending subarray-granularity mitigations triples perceived activations and costs 17% slowdown - independently confirms that the cost-model consequence, not the phenomenon alone, is what the community picked up
	6. **impact boundary - where this does NOT apply**:
		1. **DRAM arrays that are not open-bitline.** The three-subarray span is a direct consequence of the open-bitline architecture's shared bitlines (Sec. 2.1, Obs. 4-5). A folded-bitline or otherwise non-sharing organization would have a different - possibly single-subarray - victim set, and the paper's structural argument does not transfer
		2. **4F-squared VCT DRAM**, where a bitline-mediated disturbance also exists but with a different device architecture and a believed-different mechanism (floating body effect) (Sec. 7)
		3. **DDR5 / LPDDR / GDDR**, untested; the measured amplification factors do not transfer as stated, and DDR5's mandatory on-die ECC changes what software observes (though Obs. 27 suggests not for the better)
		4. **systems already refreshing aggressively enough** that no ColumnDisturb bitflip manifests - which is precisely mitigation 1, at 42.1% throughput cost
		5. **security severity is unestablished.** With no attack, no real-system demonstration, and no privilege-escalation path, the paper establishes a *reliability* failure mode and a *capability*, not a measured security risk
		6. non-DRAM memory technologies
10. risks (Heilmeier)
	1. **adoption realism**:
		1. the victim set is structural and huge. Any mitigation must either refresh ~3072 rows per aggressor amortized over the threshold window, or find a coarser-granularity trick. This is a genuinely harder engineering problem than RowHammer's, and the paper's own PRVR is a sketch
		2. **no threshold exists to configure against.** The paper reports times-to-first-bitflip, not a standardized activation threshold, and no vendor publishes one. [INFERENCE] this is a specification-level blocker: the mitigation cannot be parameterized until JEDEC or the vendors define the quantity - the same gap RowPress hit
		3. mitigation 1 is deployable today and costs 42.1% throughput / 67.5% refresh energy. That is the *only* option available for already-shipped hardware, and it is close to unusable - so every DDR4/HBM2 part in the field stays effectively unmitigated
		4. [INFERENCE] split ownership again: a real fix needs either DRAM chip changes (vendor) or controller changes (CPU vendor), and neither party owns the whole problem - the dynamic that made RowHammer take a decade
		5. [INFERENCE] the retention-aware-refresh finding creates an awkward incentive: the mechanisms whose benefits ColumnDisturb erases were never widely deployed in the first place, so the loudest system-level result lands mostly on an academic literature rather than on shipping products. The result that would move vendors - an attack - is the one the paper does not have
	2. **evidence gaps** (what a reviewer attacks first):

		3. **no device model**, so the "worsens with scaling" extrapolation is trend-fitting over a die-revision proxy variable rather than physics

12. my take
	1. **Correctness - strong, and for a specific reason: the paper argues from array structure, not from a curve.** The central claim could have been established badly (sweep something, observe bitflips, name it). Instead the open-bitline hypothesis makes three predictions that had no independent reason to hold - exactly three subarrays affected and no others, 1.45x more bitflips in the aggressor's own subarray (all bitlines vs half), and **zero column-index overlap between the two neighboring subarrays' bitflipping cells** - and all three hold. The third is the single most convincing datum in the paper; no retention or RowHammer story predicts a column-index disjointness between two subarrays
	2. **The methodology is the other reason to believe it, and it is worth stealing.** Two known phenomena are subtracted before anything is claimed: retention via a five-pattern, 50-repeat, lowest-observed-time filter, and RowHammer/RowPress via an 8-row guardband four times wider than the +/-1 rows where the authors actually see them. Add refresh disabled, ECC-free parts verified, in-DRAM row mapping reverse engineered, bisection to 1% rather than fixed steps, five repeats, 46,080 subarrays. The **RowClone-as-microscope** trick for finding subarray boundaries (Sec. 3.2) is the cleverest single piece of instrumentation here - an in-DRAM copy primitive repurposed to reveal array structure the interface hides
	3. **The best analytical move is Sec. 4.6.** Collapsing data pattern, aggressor-on-time, and access pattern into a single AVG(V_COL) variable turns four sweeps into one explanation, and then Sec. 5.3's two-aggressor experiment *tests a prediction of that model* rather than adding a fifth sweep. Most characterization papers never get past enumerating sensitivities; this one builds a predictor and then risks it. That is the difference between a measurement paper and a scientific one
	4. **Promise vs delivery.** The abstract makes six promises. (i) a new widespread column-based phenomenon - delivered emphatically, 216/216 chips. (ii) worsens with technology scaling - delivered, but only under the die-revision proxy. (iii) bitflips within the standard refresh window in existing chips - delivered as an existence proof on **one module**, which the body states honestly and the abstract compresses. (iv) up to 198x more rows than retention failures - delivered, at refresh intervals far past nominal. (v) strong implications for retention-aware refresh - delivered, and it is the best-supported system claim in the paper. (vi) "describe and evaluate two solutions" - **half-delivered**: mitigation 1 is evaluated and rejected on its own cost, while PRVR is an analytical estimate under an assumed threshold, with system integration explicitly out of scope. That sixth promise is where a program committee pushes hardest, and it is the section furthest below the standard the characterization sets
	5. **The most underrated result is Sec. 6.2, and the most under-defended is Sec. 6.1.** The RAIDR study is the paper's real teeth: it takes a well-studied optimization class and shows the *premise* is wrong, not the implementation - a 99-percentage-point benefit erasure for the low-area variant. That is a harder and more useful result than "here is a new bug". Meanwhile Sec. 6.1 prices its mitigations on a DRAM generation the paper never measured, using a threshold it never observed
	6. **The 1-to-0-only directionality deserves more worry than the paper gives it.** In RowPress, the directionality *reversal* was the cleanest separator from RowHammer. Here the direction is the *same* as retention failures - the confound the entire methodology is built to exclude. That inverts the epistemic situation: the retention filter is not one safeguard among several, it is the single load-bearing element separating the phenomenon from the null hypothesis, and its adequacy depends on VRT's tail being covered by 50 repeats. The structural evidence (Obs. 4-5) is what actually rescues the argument, since retention failures have no reason to respect subarray boundaries or column-index partitions. The paper never quite says this out loud
	7. **Clarity - very good.** 27 boxed observations, 12 takeaways, a boxed Key Hypothesis, consistent per-manufacturer figure grammar with error bands, and vendors named rather than anonymized (an improvement on the field's habit, including RowPress's own "Mfrs. A/B/C" slip). Nits: the observation density makes stretches of Secs. 4-5 read as a lab notebook; several headline abstract numbers come from operating-point corners the reader must trace back to find; and fn. 12 defers two of three metrics for most experiments to the extended version, so the camera-ready is thinner than its observation count suggests
	8. **Overclaim watch**: "ColumnDisturb induces bitflips within a standard DDR4 refresh window (e.g., in 63.6 ms) in multiple cells from one module" is scrupulously accurate in the intro and gets compressed in the abstract. "We describe and evaluate two solutions" is generous for a pair where one is evaluated only to be rejected and the other is not evaluated at all. The 198x, 152.66x, and 5.06x figures are all real and all corners
	9. would I accept it? Yes. It identifies a genuinely new failure mode, establishes it by structural prediction rather than correlation, characterizes it across every chip it could get, hands the community a system-level consequence with a number attached, and is candid that it cannot explain the physics. The mitigation section is thin - and the field's response (two independent mitigation papers within a year, plus Top Picks 2026) is the strongest evidence that thinness was the right call: the paper's job was to establish the phenomenon and the cost model, and it did
13. relevance
	1. this is the **canonical citation for "read disturbance is subarray-scoped, not row-adjacency-scoped"**. Any mitigation I design or review now has to declare its victim-set scope as an explicit parameter, and this paper supplies both the vocabulary and the 3072-row number that makes the old scoping indefensible
	2. it is the **second axis added to the same defense stack** that [[RowPress- Amplifying Read Disturbance in Modern DRAM Chips]] widened. RowPress said the *count* threshold is wrong; ColumnDisturb says the *scope* is wrong. Together they mean a mitigation must be specified on three dimensions - activation count, row-open time, and victim-set extent - and no deployed mechanism states all three
	3. directly reusable for my work in four ways:
		1. as a **methodology template**: subtract every known phenomenon with a stated, over-wide guardband before claiming a new one; reverse-engineer the hidden structure with an existing primitive (RowClone) rather than assuming it; build one collapsing variable (AVG(V_COL)) and then test a prediction of it instead of adding another sweep
		2. as a **structural-argument template** transferable outside DRAM: when a shared physical resource connects components the abstraction says are unrelated, the sharing topology *predicts* the fault map - and a topology-matching fault map is far stronger evidence than any single correlation
		3. as a **foil**: the DDR5/on-die-ECC gap, the missing device model, the unevaluated PRVR, the absent real-system demonstration, the missing composed-adversary study, and the metric-without-a-threshold problem are six separable open problems, of which the field has so far taken only the mitigation one (ColumnKeeper, CODA)
		4. as a **cost-model argument**: "the mechanism is fine, the scope parameter is off by 1000x" is a reusable way to attack a mature defense literature without needing a new attack
	4. the retention-aware refresh result (Sec. 6.2) is the part I would build on first - it is a worked example of falsifying a literature's *premise* rather than its mechanisms, and the same move applies anywhere a design exploits measured heterogeneity that a second, unmodeled process can erase

16. terms
	1. **read disturbance**: accessing one DRAM location perturbs charge in physically nearby cells that were not accessed. The umbrella phenomenon; RowHammer, RowPress, and now ColumnDisturb are its members
	2. **ColumnDisturb**: this paper's phenomenon - hammering or pressing an aggressor row disturbs cells *sharing its columns (bitlines)*, across three consecutive subarrays. Victims are columns, not row-neighbors
	3. **RowHammer** [2, 3] / **RowPress** [4]: read disturbance from repeatedly activating a row, and from keeping a row open a long time. Both affect only a few rows adjacent to the aggressor within one subarray
	4. **aggressor row / victim row**: the row being accessed, and a row whose bits flip as a side effect
	5. **subarray**: a two-dimensional block of DRAM cells (1024 rows in the tested chips) interconnected by bitlines and wordlines, with its own sense amplifiers. The unit ColumnDisturb's blast radius is measured in
	6. **bitline (column) / wordline (row)**: the vertical line connecting a column of cells to a sense amplifier, and the horizontal line that turns on a row's access transistors. In this paper "DRAM column" means one physical bitline (fn. 1)
	7. **open-bitline architecture** [92-100]: the high-density layout in which each subarray's bitlines connect to two rows of sense amplifiers, one above and one below, so a subarray shares its *even* bitlines with the subarray above and its *odd* bitlines with the one below. **The structural fact that makes ColumnDisturb span three subarrays and gives the two neighbors disjoint column indices**
	8. **sense amplifier**: the circuit that detects and restores the small charge difference a cell puts on its bitline. Shared between neighboring subarrays under open-bitline
	9. **true-cell / anti-cell**: whether a fully charged cell encodes 1 (true) or 0 (anti). Because leakage drains charge, true cells storing 1 are the ones that fail to 0
	10. **ACT / PRE**: activate (drive a wordline, transfer the row to the sense amplifiers) and precharge (close the row, restore bitlines to VDD/2)
	11. **tRAS / tRP**: minimum ACT-to-PRE time (the nominal minimum row-open time, 36 ns here) and minimum PRE-to-ACT time (14 ns here)
	12. **tAggOn**: aggressor row on time - how long the row stays open after each activation. Swept at 36 ns, 7.8 us, 70.2 us, and 1 ms; 70.2 us is the default because it is the most vulnerable and is the largest JEDEC-legal row-open time
	13. **tREFI / tREFW / REF**: refresh interval (7.8 us for DDR4), refresh window (64 ms), and the refresh command. **REFab** is the all-bank refresh whose period Sec. 6.1 shortens
	14. **retention failure**: a cell losing its charge before the next refresh, with no disturbance involved. ColumnDisturb's baseline comparison *and* its principal confound, since both flip 1 to 0
	15. **VRT (variable retention time)** [85, 86, 115, 116]: a cell's retention time changing over time, which is why the retention filter repeats each test 50 times and uses the lowest observed value
	16. **blast radius** [3, 4, 7, 47, 50, 71, ...]: the number of rows in a subarray experiencing at least one bitflip. Up to all 1024 rows of a subarray here, versus +/-1 for RowHammer
	17. **AVG(V_COL) / DP_COL**: the time-weighted average voltage on the perturbed columns, and the voltage the aggressor's data pattern holds them at. AVG(V_COL) = (tAggOn x DP_COL + VDD/2 x tRP) / (tAggOn + tRP). **The paper's unifying explanatory variable** - lower means more vulnerable
	18. **subthreshold leakage / dielectric leakage**: current through a nominally-off access transistor, and current through the insulator between the cell capacitor and the bitline. The two candidates in the paper's Key Hypothesis
	19. **BCAT (Buried Channel Access Transistor)** [109, 110] / **GIDL (Gate-Induced Drain Leakage)**: the modern DRAM cell transistor structure and one of its major leakage paths; background for why true cells storing 1 leak toward 0
	20. **DRAM Bender** [118, 119] / **SoftMC** [120, 121]: the open-source FPGA-based infrastructure that issues arbitrary DDR4/HBM2 command sequences with precise timing. What makes a 70.2 us tAggOn expressible at all
	21. **RowClone** [90]: an in-DRAM row copy performed by two consecutive activations within a subarray. Used here **as a measurement instrument** - if a copy succeeds, source and destination share a subarray, which reveals the subarray boundaries
	22. **retention-aware heterogeneous refresh** [75-88]: refresh weak rows often and strong rows rarely, exploiting retention-time heterogeneity. The mechanism class ColumnDisturb undermines
	23. **RAIDR** [76]: the canonical retention-aware refresh mechanism, using Bloom filters to bin rows by retention time. Evaluated here in Bloom-filter and bitmap variants
	24. **weak row / strong row**: a row with a cell failing within a short window (e.g. 64 ms), versus one reliable over a long window (e.g. 1024 ms). Retention-aware refresh's benefit is a function of the strong-row proportion, which ColumnDisturb collapses
	25. **SMD (Self-Managing DRAM)** [248, 249] / **Ramulator** [250-253]: the DRAM-maintenance framework and the cycle-level simulator used for the Sec. 6.2 evaluation
	26. **SECDED** [163, 168-171] / **SEC Hamming (136,128)** [160]: single-error-correct double-error-detect system-level ECC, and the low-cost single-error-correcting code plausibly used as on-die ECC in DDR5. Both defeated here; the SEC code additionally *miscorrects* 88.5% of two-bitflip codewords into three-bitflip ones
	27. **on-die ECC** [134, 150, 151, 173, 174]: error correction inside the DRAM chip, invisible to the memory controller and with vendor-confidential design. Mandatory in DDR5, absent from every part tested here
	28. **tDRFMab** [148]: the DDR5 timing for a directed refresh management operation - 280 ns for +/-2 neighbor rows, 560 ns for +/-4. The standard's own price list for victim-set scope, and the basis for the ~215 us estimate of refreshing 3072 rows
	29. **PRVR (Proactively Refreshing ColumnDisturb Victim Rows)**: this paper's proposed mitigation - refresh the N victim rows across the three affected subarrays once, spread over the time-to-first-bitflip window, before a bitflip can occur
	30. **4F-squared VCT DRAM** [259-263]: an emerging high-density array using vertical channel transistors, known to suffer bitline disturbance via a floating-body mechanism. The closest prior art, explicitly distinguished from contemporary 6F-squared COTS DRAM
	31. **ColumnKeeper** / **CODA**: the two follow-on mitigation papers (ISCA 2026 and arXiv 2026 respectively). ColumnKeeper-D uses two counters per subarray tracking odd and even column activations; ColumnKeeper-P refreshes probabilistically; CODA reduces the adjacent-counter increments those schemes require
