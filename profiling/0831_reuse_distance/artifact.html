Conflict, Not Capacity

The L2 in the best config hits 0.00%. Reuse-distance measurement says its working set fits in 256 KB. Both are true, and what separates them is the set index.

Scope, added 2026-08-31

These four layers are not representative

Everything on this page was measured on V8, V9, R9 and R16, the four layers the 2026-08-23 study chose because their weight footprints spanned the L2 axis, which is to say selected for having L2 pressure. A later scan over all 31 layers of both networks found that only 5 of 31 have any L2 reuse at all. The other 26 touch every L2 line exactly once, so no associativity, no capacity and no replacement policy can change them.

Every per-layer number below stands and every check still passes. What does not survive is the extrapolation: the 12.7 to 14.5% cycle gain from more ways is real on the layers that have reuse and is 2.58% end to end across both whole networks.

The follow-on measurement is The Idle L2.

The answer

What the distances found

Friday's sweep measured an L2 hit rate of 0.00% at every point and read it as a capacity cliff: the layer's weight set is 2.25 MiB, the L2 is 512 KB, so under a cyclic sweep every line is evicted before its reuse. The prescription was a 4 MB L2.

Measuring the actual reuse distances says the sweep is not cyclic at the L2 at all. The largest distance anywhere in the corpus is 3,071 lines. The 512 KB L2 already holds 8,192. The capacity was never the problem.

The L2 is losing to conflict

At a fixed 512 KB, widening from 16-way to 64-way takes the hit rate from 0.00% to its full 52.63% ceiling and cuts 12.7–14.5% off total cycles. Same capacity, same layout, same everything else.

512 KB now equals the 4 MB prescription

512 KB at 64-way and 4 MB at 16-way reach the identical hit rate and the identical cycle count. The eight-fold capacity increase buys nothing that associativity does not.

The L1 runs the other way

Its 8-way array beats a fully-associative LRU cache of the same size, by up to 5.36 points. The kernel-indexed layout is doing precisely what it was built to do, one level down from where it was checked.

The measurement

These histograms are line spectra

The most surprising thing in the data is not any single number, it is the shape. A general workload spreads reuse distances across a broad continuum. These layers do not. Across all four layers and both levels the whole corpus lands on between 2 and 17 distinct distances: V8's entire L1 stream sits on four values and its L2 stream on two, while R9's L1 is the richest in the set at seventeen. Everything else is cold.

That is what a rigidly tiled dataflow looks like from inside the cache: the address stream is a small set of strides walked over and over, so reuse happens at a handful of exact offsets and nowhere in between.

Every bar is drawn from outputs/reuse_hist.csv, sample 0, L1 summed over its sixteen private stacks. Both axes are logarithmic, and both are forced by the data: the buckets span 0 to 3,071, and one bucket holds 84% while another holds 0.03%. Cold sits outside the frame because a first reference has no distance, so putting it anywhere on the axis would assert something false. The dashed copper line is the capacity that level actually had. Hover any bar for its exact count.

Read the two rows of each pair as two different streams, because they are. The L1 sees every demand reference and its mass sits at d=0: a burst re-touching the line it just touched, 84% of V8's traffic. The L2 sees only what the L1 missed, so d=0 never reaches it at all, and its two buckets sit at 1,023 and 3,071.

Note where the copper line falls in each row. At the L1 it sits inside the distribution, with the 191 and 287 buckets straddling it, which is a cache doing real work against a real constraint. At the L2 it sits far to the right of every bar the layer produces. Nothing in that stream is asking for more capacity than the cache already has.

Sample 0, every other parameter held at the best config. R9 is the control and behaves correctly: it has no L2 reuse to recover, so no geometry helps it. The transition is abrupt, at 32 ways: 16-way gives 0.00%, and 64, 256, 1024 and 8,192 ways all give exactly 52.63%.

Adding sets does not substitute for adding ways

Friday's own capacity sweep is the evidence. Going 512 KB to 1 MB to 2 MB at 16-way doubles the set count twice and leaves V8 at 0.00% throughout. Going 512 KB 16-way to 512 KB 64-way quarters the set count and fixes it completely.

So the lines inside a reuse window are landing in too few distinct sets, and widening the index does not separate them, because the bits the index gains are not the bits that vary within a window. That is a statement about where khkw_split puts each digit, and it is worth saying plainly that the layout was tuned against the L1's five index bits. The L2 at 512 KB has nine, and the four it adds were never checked.

The mechanism above is the hypothesis the data most obviously supports, not a measured result. Confirming it means counting the distinct L2 sets touched inside one reuse window, which the access log already contains and no run has yet asked it for.
The counter-result

At the L1, the layout is already winning

Stack distance gives an upper bound for fully-associative LRU. It is not a bound on a set-associative cache, which partitions the stream and can retain what a global LRU would evict. That usually costs a fraction of a point. Here it earns several.

The two results are one result. The kernel-indexed layout spreads a reuse window across the L1's sets so well that per-set LRU outperforms global LRU. The same index, extended to the L2's larger set count, concentrates a reuse window instead. The layout is not wrong; it is correct at one level and unexamined at the other.

Belady's MIN

What a perfect policy can and cannot rescue

The conflict result says 16-way loses reuse it has the capacity to keep. That leaves one question open: is LRU simply the wrong policy here, or are the sets genuinely over-subscribed? Belady's MIN separates them, because it evicts the line whose next use is furthest away and is therefore the best any policy could do.

The answer is both, in measurable proportions.

L2 hit rate, mean of five samples. R9 is the control throughout: it has no L2 reuse, so no policy and no geometry can move it.

LRU at 16-way is pathological, not merely suboptimal. It returns exactly zero where the optimum returns 26.32%. That is not a policy losing a few points at the margin.

And the optimum still only reaches half the ceiling. 26.32% is precisely half of 52.63%, so even perfect knowledge of the future leaves half the available reuse on the floor at 16-way. More lines are live in a set than the set has ways, and no replacement policy can hold what does not fit.

At 64-way, Belady and LRU are identical to the cycle. Once the ways are there, LRU is already optimal, so there is no policy headroom left to chase. That is the cleanest possible statement that associativity, not replacement, is the lever.

The result that outranks the hit rate

Belady at 16-way wins 26.32% of its L2 accesses and buys essentially no makespan. The hit rates are not in question: an offline simulation written independently against the engine’s own L2 reference sequence and its own line-to-set mapping reproduces 0.00% and 26.32% exactly. What needs explaining is why a quarter of the accesses turning into hits is worth nothing.

First, a correction. An earlier version of this page reported Belady as slower than LRU, 282,854 cycles becoming 286,539. That excess is not the policy: it is L2 port serialisation. With l2_ii = 1 the L2 admits one request per cycle, and under Belady a quarter of accesses return in 2 cycles instead of 26, so those cores come back to the port about thirteen times sooner and collide with the core that sets the tile’s time. Removing the port limit removes the excess and flips the sign:

The accounting is unambiguous. At a 2000-cycle DRAM latency, where the memory leg dominates everything, Belady saves 42,540,344 cycles of channel stall and gains 42,405,704 cycles of barrier stall. Those agree to 0.3%. Every cycle it saves in memory reappears as waiting.

                   LRU       Belady     delta
l2_ii = 1       290,136     293,888    +3,752   <- port artifact
l2_ii = 0       290,136     289,856      -280   <- the real policy effect

So Belady’s floor really is LRU, as an optimal policy’s must be. The residual gain is just very small, and the rest of this section is about why.

The composition changes completely and the total does not move at all. At 64-way, by contrast, the stall total itself falls by half and the makespan follows.

A simple model separates the two cases. If the makespan were set by each core's own serialised miss chain, it would be the per-core miss count times the latency:

LRU    16-way   4,864 misses/core  ->  makespan = 4,946 x latency   holds
LRU    64-way   2,304 misses/core  ->  makespan = 2,398 x latency   holds
Belady 16-way   3,536 misses/core  ->  makespan = 4,946 x latency   FAILS, 40% excess

So Belady’s misses are uniform in total per core, and every core really does get exactly 1,280 hits, yet they are not uniform across the barrier intervals. Recovering the tile for every L2 reference settles what happens, and no engine change was needed to do it: under LRU at 16-way every access misses, so the k-th L2 fill row in the cache-state log is the k-th reference’s tile, 77,824 against 77,824. Joining that with the access log’s core gives the per-core, per-tile grid, and it reproduces every measured slope to the integer.

A tile ends when its slowest core finishes, so the makespan measures the sum over tiles of the per-tile maximum, while the DRAM count measures the maximum over cores of a total. The first is never smaller. Ratio 1.000 means perfectly uniform, and only Belady at 16-way diverges.

MIN serves whole cores and starves the rest

Across the 88 tiles carrying L2 traffic:

tiles where Belady lands ZERO hits on any core:            24 of 88
tiles where Belady helps EVERY core (so the max must fall):  0 of 88
tiles whose per-tile MAXIMUM is unchanged:                  88 of 88

And the split inside a tile is bimodal rather than partial:

tile   refs/core   LRU max   MIN max   MIN min   spread
   0        128       128       128       128        0
   3         64        64        64        32       32
   7         32        32        32         0       32
  11         32        32        32         0       32

In tile 7 some cores fall from 32 misses to zero, completely served, while others stay at 32, completely starved. Never 32 to 20. MIN keeps the sixteen lines with the nearest next use in each set, and because the cores are structurally symmetric while their lines sit at different points in the global next-use ordering, “nearest sixteen” resolves to whole cores. The starved cores keep exactly LRU’s count, so the per-tile maximum is exactly LRU’s in all 88 of 88 tiles, and the makespan cannot move by a single cycle.

Associativity works for the same reason MIN fails. Sixty-four ways gives every core room at once, all sixteen fall to 2,304 together, the ratio stays at 1.000, and the critical path drops by the full factor.

The lesson generalises past this machine. Total hits and the sum of per-tile maxima are different objective functions, and optimising the first can be worth exactly zero on the second. Belady is optimal for the wrong one. A replacement policy for a barrier-synchronised array would have to be scoped to a tile and fair across cores, which is the opposite of a global optimiser.

An earlier version of this page attributed the effect to Belady helping non-critical cores. Per-core accounting refutes that outright, since all sixteen cores are identical, and it has been withdrawn.

What to change

The cheap fix and the real one

  • Raise the L2 to 64-way and keep 512 KB. Recovers the full 12.7–14.5% at one eighth the capacity Friday's reading called for. A 64-way 512 KB L2 is not free in area or access energy, but it is far cheaper than 4 MB.
  • Re-cut the L2 set index. If a different assignment of khkw_split's digits spreads a reuse window across the L2's 512 sets, 16-way keeps the win and costs nothing. This is the result worth having, and the access log already holds the data to test it.
  • Do not chase the replacement policy. Belady's MIN reaches only half the ceiling at 16-way, and at 64-way it is identical to LRU to the cycle. There is no policy headroom worth engineering at either geometry.
  • Optimise per tile, not per layer. Belady's extra hits turned into barrier slack cycle for cycle and bought no speedup. Under a lock-step barrier the figure of merit is the sum over tiles of the slowest core, and total hit rate is a proxy that can move the wrong way. A policy tuned for this machine should be scoped to a tile.
  • Re-open the prefetch question. Friday found prefetching a 37–90% penalty at 64-byte lines, measured against an L2 that was returning 0.00%. With the L2 actually hitting, the arithmetic behind that verdict changes and it deserves a re-run.

What does not change: 64-byte lines still win outright, L2 banking is still inert, and prefetch distance 0 is still the best of the four prefetch settings that were swept.

Method

How the distances were obtained

Reuse distance here is LRU stack distance in distinct lines: for a reference to line X, the count of distinct lines touched since the previous reference to X. A reference hits in a fully-associative LRU cache of N lines exactly when its distance is below N, so one pass yields the hit rate at every capacity. A first reference is cold and gets its own bucket.

The engine writes the demand reference stream as a fixed-width binary log, emitted at the same two sites that increment l1_accesses and l2_accesses, which is what makes V1 an identity rather than a coincidence. A standalone tool then walks it with a Fenwick tree over the timestamp axis, about 19 slot touches per reference against roughly 2,300 for a backward scan.

Seventeen independent stacks per run: one per private L1, one for the shared L2. All 24 runs complete in 12 seconds.

for each reference to line X at time t:
    if X unseen:   record COLD
    else:          d = prefix(t-1) - prefix(p);  remove marker at p
    place marker at t;  last[X] = t

The naive backward scan is kept as the test oracle and never as a production path: 850 checks agree between the two, across the worked example, 2,000 random sequences, every length around a power of two, and a synthetic cyclic sweep.
